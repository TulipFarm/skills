# Financial Statements

Turn a clean set of books into the three statements that actually describe a business,
then say in plain words what they reveal.

## When to use

Use this skill when asked to prepare, assemble, or explain an income statement (P&L),
a balance sheet, or a cash-flow statement for a period. Assume the underlying ledger is
already recorded and reconciled — this is the formal reporting layer, not day-to-day
categorization or forward forecasting.

## How to produce

1. **Confirm the period and the basis first.** State the date range and whether the
   books are cash or accrual. The two bases produce different numbers; never mix them
   silently in one statement.
2. **Build the income statement top-down.** Revenue, then cost of goods/services to get
   gross profit, then operating expenses to get operating income, then interest and tax
   to get net income. Show subtotals — a P&L without gross and operating margins hides
   the story.
3. **Build the balance sheet as a snapshot.** Assets (current then long-term),
   liabilities (current then long-term), then equity. It must balance: assets =
   liabilities + equity. If it doesn't, stop and find the error before reporting.
4. **Build the cash-flow statement in three sections.** Operating, investing, financing.
   Reconcile the start and end cash balances — the ending cash here must match the cash
   line on the balance sheet.
5. **Tie the statements together.** Net income flows into equity; cash flow explains the
   change in the cash balance. If they don't reconcile, the books aren't ready.

## Reading what stands out

- Lead with three or four observations, not a number dump: margin trend, whether the
  business is cash-generating or cash-burning, leverage, and anything unusual versus the
  prior period.
- Quantify each point ("gross margin fell from 42% to 36%, driven by COGS") rather than
  vague adjectives.
- Flag a profitable-but-cash-poor or cash-rich-but-unprofitable split explicitly — it is
  the most common thing owners miss.
- This is reporting and interpretation, not professional financial or tax advice; route
  filing or financing decisions to a qualified professional.
