# Document Drafting

Write internal documents that help a reader understand, decide, or act. The draft must
distinguish source evidence from interpretation and never hide missing information.

## When to use

Use this skill for internal memos, proposals, briefings, business cases, executive summaries,
and decision papers. Do not use it for marketing copy, product specifications, engineering
documentation, or step-by-step operating procedures.

## How to draft a document

1. **Define the reader and decision.** Name the primary audience, what they already know,
   what they need from the document, and the action or decision it should support.
2. **Set the document contract.** Confirm type, length, tone, deadline, required sections,
   and any constraints on confidentiality or distribution.
3. **Inventory the evidence.** List source material with dates and owners. Separate facts,
   estimates, opinions, and assumptions before writing.
4. **Choose a decision-shaped structure.** Lead with the purpose and main point, then present
   context, evidence, options, recommendation, risks, and next steps as needed.
5. **Draft claims from sources.** Cite important facts where they appear. Do not strengthen
   tentative source language or invent data to fill a gap.
6. **Make reasoning visible.** Explain how the evidence supports conclusions. State material
   assumptions and show where a different assumption would change the recommendation.
7. **Write for scanning.** Use informative headings, short paragraphs, concrete labels,
   and tables only when comparison is easier in rows and columns.
8. **Address objections and risk.** Include the strongest reasonable counterpoint, key
   dependencies, downside exposure, and mitigation options.
9. **Check internal consistency.** Verify names, dates, totals, definitions, recommendations,
   and action owners across the full draft.
10. **Prepare for review.** Mark unresolved questions and requested reviewer decisions.
    Require human approval before publishing, distributing, or replacing an existing document.

## Quality bar

- The opening states why the document exists and what the reader should do.
- Every material factual claim traces to supplied evidence.
- Assumptions, estimates, and uncertainties are labeled.
- The recommendation follows from the evidence and addresses credible alternatives.
- The draft is complete enough to review, with gaps collected rather than buried.
- Publication or distribution remains a human-confirmed action.
