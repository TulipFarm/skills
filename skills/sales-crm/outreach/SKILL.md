# Outreach

Write messages a busy stranger will actually answer: specific to them, short enough to
read on a phone, and asking for exactly one thing.

## When to use

Use this skill when asked to draft a cold or warm email, a connection note, or a
follow-up sequence to a prospect. It assumes you already know who you are writing to —
if you need to gather that context first, do the research, then come back here.

## How to draft

1. **Open with them, not you.** The first line must reference something specific — a
   trigger event, their role, a shared connection. A generic opener ("Hope you're doing
   well!") gets deleted before the second sentence.
2. **Earn the message with one relevant insight.** Tie what you do to a pain they
   plausibly have, in one sentence. Sell the conversation, not the product. Lead with
   their problem, not your feature list.
3. **Make exactly one ask, and make it small.** One clear call to action per message — a
   short reply, fifteen minutes, a yes/no. Two asks halve your response rate; a big ask
   kills it. "Worth a quick chat?" beats "Book a 60-minute demo."
4. **Cut every line that isn't working.** Aim for under 90 words. No buzzwords, no
   throat-clearing, no paragraph about your company. If a sentence doesn't move them
   toward the ask, delete it.
5. **Sequence the follow-ups to add value, not nag.** 3-5 touches over ~2-3 weeks, each
   from a fresh angle: a new insight, a relevant example, a short "should I close the
   loop?" Never just "bumping this." Each touch must stand on its own.
6. **Write like a person.** Match a plain spoken voice, one idea per sentence, a real
   sign-off. If it reads like a template, it will be treated like one.

## Output format

```
Outreach Sequence — <prospect>, <channel>
Touch 1 (Day 0): subject: <line>
  <body — opener, insight, one ask>
Touch 2 (Day 3): <new angle, one ask>
Touch 3 (Day 7): <example or proof, one ask>
Touch 4 (Day 14): <break-up / close-the-loop>
```

Keep each touch under 90 words and to a single ask. Personalize the variable bits —
never ship a sequence that could go to anyone.
